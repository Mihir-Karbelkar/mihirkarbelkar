For most AI startups, the model bill is the biggest cost nobody planned for. Salaries are budgeted a year ahead. Inference grows with every user, every agent step and every retry.

It usually goes the same way. The prototype runs on the best frontier API, because quality matters and volume is tiny. Then usage grows, and the same model handles every task, simple ones included. The bill now scales with traffic rather than with value, and margins that looked like software start to look like services.

If nobody keeps it in check, it comes for you. That is not a guess: below are three startups it happened to, then a longer list, each with a source.

## Three startups that hit the wall

### Character.AI: popular, and losing money on every user

In October 2026, Character.AI filed for Chapter 11. It had millions of monthly users, and people spent hours a day talking to its characters. According to [404 Media's reading of the filing](https://www.404media.co/character-ais-bankruptcy-documents-show-the-economics-of-ai-dont-work/):

- Compute cost about $13.8M a month in 2024 and about $10M a month in 2025.
- From January to August 2026, it spent $56.1M on compute and $19.7M on salaries and benefits.
- Revenue, ads included, peaked at $5.3M a month. Subscriptions brought in $2.8M in their best month.
- After cuts, ads and cheaper GPUs, it was still losing $8–9M a month in August.

The filing also blames legal costs, but by 404 Media's reading the business did not work even without them. Disney has offered $20M for some of its IP ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/13/character-ai-chapter-11-disney-bid.html)).

Every conversation cost money to serve, and the product earned less per user than it cost to serve them. More users made the hole deeper, not shallower.

### AI Dungeon: the bill grew with every player

AI Dungeon, from the startup Latitude, ran its stories on OpenAI's GPT models. [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/03/13/chatgpt-and-generative-ai-are-booming-but-at-a-very-expensive-price.html) that the more people played, the bigger the bill Latitude had to pay OpenAI. By the end of 2021, it had moved to a cheaper model from AI21 Labs.

This one survived, because it changed models before the bill decided for it. Most teams only make that switch once it is urgent.

### Stability AI: compute nine times its revenue

The company behind Stable Diffusion was on track for about $99M a year in compute against about $11M of revenue in 2023. It reportedly fell behind on payments to AWS, Google Cloud and CoreWeave, cash dropped to about $4M by October 2023, and the CEO resigned in March 2024 ([The Register, summarising Forbes](https://www.theregister.com/2024/04/03/stability_ai_bills/)).

## Why the bill grows

It is usually down to how the system is built, not what the provider charges. Four habits cause most of it:

- **One model for every task.** The frontier model picked for hard reasoning also does classification, extraction and summarisation, and those make up most of the volume.
- **Agents multiply calls.** One user action turns into planning, tool calls, retries and self-checks. Each is a full model call, and the context gets longer every time.
- **No per-task cost view.** Teams see one monthly invoice, not which step of which pipeline spends the money.
- **Context bloat.** Full documents, long system prompts and chat history are resent on every call, so token counts grow faster than traffic.

## An example startup stack

Here is a made-up but typical case: an AI support product. Each customer question runs through five model calls, all on the same frontier API:

| Step      | What it does                                           | Needs a frontier model? |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| Classify  | Which product, and how urgent                          | No                      |
| Rerank    | Picks the right help articles                          | No                      |
| Draft     | Writes the reply                                       | Sometimes               |
| Check     | Is the reply backed by the articles and within policy? | No                      |
| Summarise | Writes the note on the ticket                          | No                      |

Four of the five steps are narrow and repetitive, and they are billed at the frontier price anyway. At 10,000 questions a day, that is 50,000 model calls a day. The calculator below starts from this example.

## Paying per call against paying per hour

Most AI products buy their model the way many apps buy compute on AWS Lambda: you pay for each call, and nothing else. At low volume that is the right choice. When nobody uses the product, you pay nothing, and there are no servers to run.

The catch is that the price of a call never drops as you grow. Ten times the users means ten times the bill. Teams that ran large, steady workloads on Lambda learned this and moved them to EC2, a server rented by the hour. It costs the same whether it is busy or idle. At low volume that is wasted money. At high volume each extra request costs almost nothing, because the server is already paid for.

A model API works like Lambda. A small model on a GPU you rent works like EC2. Think of a taxi against a leased car: the taxi is cheaper if you drive once a week, the lease if you drive all day.

Owning the servers is not a cure on its own. Character.AI ran its own models on GPUs it paid for, and in 2025 still spent nearly twice its best month's revenue on compute. A fixed-cost setup only wins when the servers stay busy with traffic that earns more than it costs to serve.

The usual answer is not "move everything". It is to keep paying per call for the low-volume and hard steps, and to put the high-volume, repetitive steps on a fixed-cost model once they are past the crossover.

## Give each task the smallest model that does it

Keep the frontier model for the steps that need it, and move the high-volume, narrow steps to small models you control.

1. **Measure per task.** Log tokens and cost for each pipeline step for two weeks. A few steps usually carry most of the spend.
2. **Build an eval set first.** Sample real inputs and current outputs for each high-volume step. That is the bar any replacement has to meet.
3. **Fine-tune a small open model.** For classification, extraction and summarisation, a fine-tuned 7–8B model often matches or beats a general frontier model on that one task, as Checkr found.
4. **Self-host where the volume justifies it.** Serve on rented GPUs with vLLM, batch aggressively and cache repeated prompts. Below a certain volume, a cheaper hosted model is the better choice.
5. **Route, don't replace.** Send easy cases to the small model, and fall back to the frontier model when its confidence is low.
6. **Gate the switch.** Nothing goes to production until it passes the eval set, and you keep monitoring after the switch.

How much you save depends on the mix. Pipelines that are mostly bulk text processing have the most room. If the spend is mostly scraping, storage or hard reasoning, there is much less.
